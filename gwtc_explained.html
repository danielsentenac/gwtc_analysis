1Viewing angle vs inclination

They describe the same geometry. The viewing angle is a folded version of the inclination (counterpart.py, viewing_angle()):

def viewing_angle(samples: dict) -> Optional[np.ndarray]:
    for k in ("theta_jn", "iota"):
        if k in samples:
            th = np.degrees(np.asarray(samples[k], float))
            return np.minimum(th, 180 - th)
    return None

Inclination is the raw parameter-estimation sample, from 0 to 180°. The posterior files provide one of two parameters:

  • theta_jn: the angle between the total angular momentum J and the line of sight. Used first.
  • iota: the angle between the orbital angular momentum L and the line of sight, at the reference frequency. Used only if theta_jn is missing.

For low-spin binary neutron stars like GW170817, J and L point almost the same way, so the two are nearly equal. When spins precess, they differ.

Viewing angle is min(θ, 180° − θ), from 0 to 90° (0 = face-on, 90 = edge-on). Folding drops the difference between seeing the binary from above (θ near 0°) and from below (θ near 180°). That difference matters for the gravitational-wave polarization, but not for:

  • the distance–inclination degeneracy, since the signal amplitude depends on |cos θ|;
  • electromagnetic constraints such as a jet opening angle, quoted relative to the jet axis on either side.

So --viewing-angle MEAN SIGMA takes a value from 0 to 90° (checked in h0_bright.py). It is compared with the folded samples, which become weights in viewing_angle_weights().

The "distance–inclination degeneracy" wording (plot titles, distance_inclination_{event}.png, notes) is the standard name from the literature; the plotted axis is the folded viewing angle.

2Recession velocity

The recession velocity is how fast the host galaxy appears to move away from us, measured from the redshift of its spectrum: v_r ≈ c·z. In the code it is the CMB-frame velocity of the host (Counterpart.v_recession).

It is the sum of two parts:

  • Hubble flow (v_H): the part due to the expansion of the universe. Only this part follows Hubble's law, v_H = H0 · d.
  • Peculiar velocity (v_p): the galaxy's own motion through space, caused by the gravity of nearby structure (groups, clusters).

For a distant host the peculiar velocity is negligible. For a nearby host it is not: NGC 4993, the host of GW170817, is about 40 Mpc away, so its peculiar velocity is roughly 10% of its total velocity. hubble_flow_redshift() removes it, adding the uncertainties in quadrature:

vh, sv = vr - vp, float(np.hypot(sr, sp))
return vh / C_KMS, sv / C_KMS, ...

With the values registered for NGC 4993, which match the GW170817 standard-siren H0 paper (Abbott et al. 2017, Nature):

Quantity Value
v_r (recession, CMB frame) 3327 ± 72 km/s
⟨v_p⟩ (peculiar) 310 ± 150 km/s
v_H = v_r − v_p 3017 ± 166 km/s, so z_H ≈ 0.01006

This Hubble-flow redshift is combined with the gravitational-wave distance to give H0. The peculiar-velocity uncertainty (±150 km/s) is a large part of the redshift error, more than the measured velocity's (±72 km/s).

CLI options

  • --v-recession V SIGMA and --v-peculiar V SIGMA override the registered values, in km/s.
  • --redshift Z SIGMA skips the velocity correction and uses the given redshift directly. Use it for a distant host with a spectroscopic redshift, where the peculiar velocity doesn't matter.
  • --v-peculiar on its own fails if the host is registered by its redshift, because there is no recession velocity to subtract it from.

3The CMB frame

Definition

The CMB frame is the reference frame where the cosmic microwave background looks the same in every direction, apart from its tiny intrinsic fluctuations. It is the closest thing to a cosmic rest frame: an observer at rest there is moving only with the expansion of the universe.

We are not at rest in it. The Sun moves at about 370 km/s relative to the CMB, toward galactic (l, b) ≈ (264°, 48°), in Crater/Leo. We see this as the CMB dipole: the CMB is about 3.4 mK hotter in that direction and colder in the opposite one.

It is a state of motion, not a place

The CMB frame is defined at every point in the universe. At any location it is the frame of a comoving observer: one with no velocity of their own, only carried along by the expansion.

Why the CMB looks the same in all directions to such an observer:

  • The CMB fills the whole universe almost uniformly. It was released everywhere at once, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang (z ≈ 1100), when the universe became transparent.
  • Wherever you are, you receive photons from a sphere around you, the last-scattering surface. Each observer has their own sphere. The early universe was very nearly uniform (the cosmological principle), so every such sphere looks nearly identical: 2.725 K in every direction, with fluctuations of about 10⁻⁵.

When you move, the Doppler effect changes this. Photons from ahead are blueshifted and those from behind redshifted, so T(θ) ≈ T₀ (1 + (v/c) cos θ). For v ≈ 370 km/s, v/c ≈ 1.23 × 10⁻³ and ΔT ≈ 3.4 mK. That is the measured dipole.

Analogy: rain on a windless day falls vertically and evenly when you stand still. Run, and it seems to come from the front and hits you harder. The rain hasn't changed, only your motion through it.

An observer at rest in the CMB frame at our position would move at about 370 km/s relative to the Sun, in the direction opposite to the apex. For that observer the dipole would vanish.

For galaxy velocities

  • Each galaxy has its own local CMB frame. Its peculiar velocity is its motion relative to it.
  • A galaxy with no peculiar velocity moves only with the Hubble flow.
  • A CMB-frame recession velocity is the cz we would measure if we were comoving observers ourselves, so our own 370 km/s is removed.
  • Subtracting the galaxy's peculiar velocity (v_H = v_r − v_p) then leaves only the Hubble flow, which is what H0 relates to distance.

The code uses the CMB frame because Hubble's law describes motion relative to the cosmic rest frame, and because the peculiar velocity it subtracts (from velocity-field reconstructions) is also given in that frame. v_H = v_r − v_p only makes sense if both are in the same frame. If you pass --v-recession yourself, give a CMB-frame value: a heliocentric velocity biases H0 by a few hundred km/s.

Where to find CMB-frame velocities

Catalogs and tools:

  • NED (see section 6): each galaxy's "Velocity corrections" table has a "CMB" row, and its velocity-correction calculator converts a heliocentric velocity to every frame.
  • HyperLEDA (leda.univ-lyon1.fr): the v3k parameter is the velocity relative to the CMB.
  • Cosmicflows-4 / Extragalactic Distance Database (edd.ifa.hawaii.edu): CMB-frame velocities plus measured and reconstructed peculiar velocities, useful for --v-peculiar.
  • Group catalogs (2MRS groups of Crook et al. 2007, Tully 2015): CMB-frame velocities averaged over a galaxy's group.

To compute it yourself, use the Planck 2018 dipole: 369.82 ± 0.11 km/s toward galactic (l, b) = (264.02°, 48.25°). To first order:

from astropy.coordinates import SkyCoord
import astropy.units as u, numpy as np

gal = SkyCoord(197.4487*u.deg, -23.3839*u.deg)              # NGC 4993
apex = SkyCoord(l=264.021*u.deg, b=48.253*u.deg, frame="galactic")
v_helio = 2930.0                                            # km/s, from the spectrum
v_cmb = v_helio + 369.82 * np.cos(gal.separation(apex))

For NGC 4993 this gives cos ≈ 0.84 and v_CMB ≈ 3241 km/s for the galaxy itself. The registered 3327 ± 72 km/s is, as far as I recall, the CMB-frame velocity of NGC 4993's galaxy group from the GW170817 H0 paper; averaging over the group reduces the effect of the galaxy's own motion within it (check the paper's references for the exact source). The value passed to --v-recession and the --v-peculiar subtracted from it should be in the same frame and describe the same object, either the galaxy or its group.

4How Planck corrects for its own motion

Planck's motion around the Sun (orbital dipole). Planck orbited L2, so it moved with the Earth around the Sun at about 30 km/s. This adds a dipole of about 270 μK whose direction turns over the year. The orbit is known to cm/s, so the signal is predicted exactly. Planck used it as its main absolute calibrator, converting detector output into kelvin (2015 and 2018 analyses, for both LFI and HFI).

The Sun's motion relative to the CMB (solar dipole). The 370 km/s dipole (about 3.36 mK) is fitted from the maps, and the standard maps are released with it subtracted. Its amplitude and direction are the values used for the CMB-frame correction.

Subtler effects. Moving at v/c ≈ 10⁻³ also:

  • aberrates the sky slightly, pushing apparent positions toward the apex;
  • Doppler-boosts the small-scale fluctuations, making them look slightly brighter and smaller in that direction;
  • adds a small kinematic quadrupole, of order (v/c)², frequency-dependent and corrected in the processing.

In 2013 Planck measured the aberration and boosting in the fluctuations themselves and found v ≈ 384 ± 78 km/s in the same direction: an independent check that the dipole comes from our motion.

Open question. The kinematic reading of the dipole is an assumption; an intrinsic CMB dipole would look the same. Counts of distant quasars and radio sources should show the same dipole, but some studies (e.g. Secrest et al. 2021) find an amplitude about twice as large. This tension is not resolved. At about 40 Mpc, everyone uses the standard kinematic interpretation.

5The chain of motions (Milky Way, Local Group)

Our 370 km/s relative to the CMB is the vector sum of several nested motions:

Motion Speed Direction
Earth around the Sun ~30 km/s changes over the year (Planck's calibrator)
Sun relative to its local stars (Local Standard of Rest) ~20 km/s roughly toward Hercules
Sun around the Galactic centre ~230–250 km/s l = 90°, the direction of Galactic rotation
Milky Way relative to the Local Group barycentre ~80–120 km/s roughly toward M31
Local Group relative to the CMB ~620 km/s (l, b) ≈ (272°, 30°)

These don't simply add up. The Sun's rotation around the Galaxy (toward l = 90°) points roughly against the Local Group's motion (toward l ≈ 272°), so it cancels part of the 620 km/s, leaving the ~370 km/s of the Sun relative to the CMB.

No separate Milky Way correction is needed. The CMB dipole is measured directly from the Sun's position (once Planck's orbital motion is removed), so it already includes all of these motions. A single heliocentric → CMB correction covers them. The intermediate frames are still used, and NED lists them all: heliocentric, LSR, Galactocentric (GSR), Local Group and CMB.

Why the Local Group moves at 620 km/s. It is the gravity of large-scale structure:

  • the pull of the Great Attractor / Norma cluster region, near the centre of the Laniakea supercluster;
  • the pull of the more distant Shapley supercluster;
  • a push away from the underdense Local Void.

NGC 4993's 310 km/s peculiar velocity has the same origin; both numbers come from the same velocity-field reconstructions of the local universe.

6NED

NED, the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu), is run by IPAC at Caltech, funded by NASA, and public since 1990. It brings together the published data on objects outside the Milky Way: galaxies, quasars, clusters, radio and X-ray sources.

For each object it collects and cross-matches data from thousands of catalogs and papers:

  • Names and positions: all aliases (NGC 4993, ESO 508-G018, PGC 45657…) resolved to one object.
  • Redshifts and velocities: the measured heliocentric redshift, plus a "Velocity corrections" table converting it to the LSR, Galactocentric, Local Group and CMB frames. This is where to look up a --v-recession value.
  • Redshift-independent distances: Cepheids, tip of the red giant branch, Tully–Fisher, surface-brightness fluctuations, supernovae… Useful to compare with a gravitational-wave distance.
  • Photometry from radio to gamma rays, images, morphology, Galactic extinction along the line of sight, and the bibliography for the object.

Useful tools:

  • Object search by name or by position (cone search).

  • A velocity-correction calculator: coordinates and a heliocentric velocity in, each frame out.

  • A cosmology calculator (distances, lookback time).

  • An API, also reachable from Python with astroquery.ipac.ned:

    from astroquery.ipac.ned import Ned
t = Ned.query_object("NGC 4993")   # position, redshift, velocity, type...

Related databases:

  • SIMBAD (CDS, Strasbourg): objects inside and outside the Galaxy, strongest on stars. NED covers extragalactic objects only.
  • HyperLEDA (Lyon): homogenized galaxy parameters, such as the v3k CMB velocity.

Positions and redshifts are commonly checked in both NED and SIMBAD.

7The area-law mode (area_law.py)

The area-law mode does not compute the remnant mass itself. The LVK release for GW250114 (Zenodo 16877102, arXiv:2509.08054) already provides area samples, computed by the collaboration for each time window. area_law.py loads them, pools the two ringdown codes, compares the areas and scans over the windows. kerr_area() is only used for the optional full-signal (--with-imr) consistency check.

Which steps come from the paper. Almost everything: the pooling, the Gaussian significance and the scans. gwtc_analysis adds the direct probability P(A_f < A_i), and approximates the area-change distribution with random pairs. Each step below is marked.

7.0 The physics

The horizon area of a Kerr black hole of mass M and spin χ is A = 8πM²[1 + √(1 − χ²)]. Hawking's area law requires A_f ≥ A_i = A₁ + A₂.

For this to be a real test, the two areas must come from separate parts of the signal:

  • A_i, from the inspiral: parameter estimation (NRSur7dq4) on data truncated at t< before the peak gives m₁, m₂, χ₁, χ₂. There are 16 truncations, from −250 M to 0 M (M = GM_total/c³ with the detector-frame total mass; see section 8).
  • A_f, from the ringdown: data starting at t> after the peak are fit with damped sinusoids, the quasinormal modes. In Kerr, a mode's frequency and damping time depend only on (M_f, χ_f), so the fit gives the remnant mass and spin without any merger model. Ringdown times are in units of M_f, the remnant's mass. Two models are fit:
    • 220: the fundamental mode;
    • 220 + 221: adding the first overtone, which decays fast and is only valid from about 6 M_f.

The full-signal fit is not a test: its remnant comes from numerical-relativity fits, which obey the law by construction.

7.1 Pooling the two ringdown codes (pooled_remnant)

the paper Pooling in equal numbers, with the paper's seed 250114.

Two independent ringdown codes analysed the data:

  • ringdown: provided at many start times and for both models;
  • pyRing: provided only at 10.5 M_f, with the 220 mode.

The code takes n = min(N_ringdown, N_pyRing) = 2000 samples from each code, drawn without replacement with the paper's seed 250114, and concatenates them into 4000 samples. Equal numbers give the two codes equal weight, so neither dominates just because it has more samples. The fixed seed reproduces the paper's exact subsample.

7.2 Headline result

partly the paper The Gaussian significance S and its 4.4σ are the paper's; the direct probability P(A_f < A_i) is added by gwtc_analysis.

A_i at t< = −40 M is compared with the pooled A_f at 10.5 M_f, in two ways.

Gaussian significance: S = (⟨A_f⟩ − ⟨A_i⟩) / √(σ_f² + σ_i²). If both areas are Gaussian and independent, A_f − A_i is Gaussian with mean ⟨A_f⟩ − ⟨A_i⟩ and width √(σ_f² + σ_i²); S says how many widths the difference lies above 0. Only the means and standard deviations are used, because with a few thousand samples the far tails are poorly sampled. At 4.4σ the one-sided tail is about 5 × 10⁻⁶, so a few thousand samples hold essentially none of it.

Direct probability: P(A_f < A_i). The code draws 400,000 random pairs (one A_f, one A_i, with replacement) and counts the fraction where the law is violated. This measures the tails directly, with no Gaussian assumption. Its resolution is about 1/400,000 ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁶, close to the Gaussian 4.4σ level, so it is reported alongside S rather than replacing it.

7.3 Relative area change

partly the paper The distribution of (A_f − A_i)/A_i is the paper's; drawing 200,000 random pairs instead of all the pairs is an approximation of gwtc_analysis.

The code computes (A_f − A_i)/A_i over 200,000 random pairs and reports the median and the 5–95% interval. The paper uses every possible pair (N_f × N_i); random pairs give nearly the same percentiles for much less computation. The histogram is plotted against the prior on this ratio implied by the PE priors (area_change_prior.dat), to show that the data, not the prior, drive the result.

Both areas are in detector-frame masses, so the (1+z)² factor cancels in the ratio and in S.

7.4 The two scans

the paper Both scans and the three numbers drawn from them (section 7.5).

Scan over the inspiral truncation: A_f is fixed (pooled, 10.5 M_f) and A_i varies over the 16 values of t<. A later truncation keeps more of the inspiral, so A_i is measured more precisely and S rises.

Scan over the ringdown start: A_i is fixed at −40 M, and A_f varies with t> for each model.

  • These scans use the ringdown code alone, since pyRing only has 10.5 M_f. So the 220 value at 10.5 M_f in this scan differs slightly from the pooled headline.
  • A later start loses signal (the ringdown decays exponentially), so A_f widens and S falls.
  • An earlier start with 220 alone risks bias from overtones that haven't decayed yet. That's why 220 + 221 is used from 6 M_f.

8The M unit (t = −40 M, 10.5 M_f…)

8.1 What M is

In GR it's common to set G = c = 1, so a mass can be expressed as a time or a length:

  • as a time: GM/c³ ≈ 4.93 μs per solar mass;
  • as a length: GM/c² ≈ 1.477 km per solar mass. This is the MSUN_KM constant in area_law.py, which converts areas to km².

So "1 M" means GM/c³ for the binary's total mass M, and t = −40 M means 40 × GM/c³ before the peak. The peak, t = 0, is the time of maximum strain amplitude, around the merger.

8.2 "G = c = 1" does not mean G equals c

G and c don't even have the same dimensions (G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹). "G = c = 1" is a choice of units: the units of length, time and mass are picked so that each constant has the numerical value 1. Each constant then becomes a conversion factor:

  1. c = 1 links time and length. Time is measured in meters, the distance light travels in that time: 1 s ↔︎ 299,792,458 m. Speeds become dimensionless, and light has speed 1.
  2. G = 1 links mass and length. The factor G/c² converts a mass into a length: 1 kg ↔︎ G/c² = 7.42 × 10⁻²⁸ m. For the Sun: 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg × 7.42 × 10⁻²⁸ m/kg ≈ 1477 m. Dividing that length by c gives a time: 1477 m / c ≈ 4.93 μs.

With G, c and a mass M, the only time you can build is GM/c³, and the only length GM/c². Writing "40 M" means 40 × GM/c³: in SI, nothing is set to 1, it's just dimensional analysis.

Analogy: "the town is 2 hours away" uses a speed to express a distance as a time; nobody concludes that hours equal kilometers. (Imperfect, since c is a true universal constant, but the logic of a constant as a conversion factor is the same.)

Setting G = c = 1 fixes two of the three base units. The third is fixed by the problem: lengths and times are expressed in units of the binary's mass M.

8.3 What 4.93 μs corresponds to

It isn't a physical event on its own: it is the natural time scale of gravity for one solar mass, the time light takes to cross the gravitational radius GM/c² ≈ 1.477 km. Strong-gravity processes around a mass M happen on simple multiples of GM/c³:

  • Black hole size: the Schwarzschild radius is 2GM/c² ≈ 2.95 km per solar mass; light crosses it in ≈ 9.9 μs.
  • Orbits: Kepler's law T = 2π√(r³/GM), with r = n × GM/c², gives T = 2π n3/2 × GM/c³.

For a non-spinning black hole:

  • ISCO is the innermost stable circular orbit. The gravitational-wave frequency is twice the orbital frequency, so f_GW,ISCO ≈ 4400 Hz × (M_☉/M).
  • Everything scales linearly with mass: a 10 M_☉ black hole rings at about 1.2 kHz, a 100 M_☉ one at about 120 Hz, a 10⁶ M_☉ one at about 0.01 Hz (the LISA band). This is why LIGO and Virgo, sensitive to tens to hundreds of Hz, see stellar-mass black-hole mergers.

8.5 Why use M instead of seconds

Vacuum GR has no built-in scale. For a given mass ratio and spins, a binary of 10 M_☉ and one of 100 M_☉ produce the same waveform shape; the heavier one is just stretched by a factor of 10 in time. In units of M, the waveform depends only on the mass ratio and spins:

  • Numerical-relativity simulations, and surrogates like NRSur7dq4, are computed with M = 1 and rescaled to the physical mass.
  • Times in M mean the same thing for every event: −40 M is always at the same stage of the late inspiral, whatever the mass.
  • The ringdown scales with M_f. The 220 damping time is about 12 M_f for this remnant's spin (value from memory; it depends on the spin), so starting at 10.5 M_f means about one damping time after the peak. By then the overtones, which decay several times faster, have mostly died out. That's why the 220 + 221 model is needed to start as early as 6 M_f.

9What σ (sigma) means in the results

In gwtc_analysis, σ is always a standard deviation, but it's used in two ways.

9.1 σ as a width: the uncertainty of one measurement

σ_f and σ_i in the area-law formula are the standard deviations of the posterior samples (final.std() and initial.std() in gaussian_significance):

σ = √( (1/N) Σ (A_k − ⟨A⟩)² )

The samples are draws from the Bayesian posterior, the probability distribution of the parameter given the data. σ measures the width of that distribution, so it is the measurement uncertainty: a small σ means the data pin the parameter down tightly.

For a Gaussian distribution:

Range Probability inside
±1σ 68.3%
±1.645σ 90%
±2σ 95.4%
±3σ 99.7%

GW papers, and most outputs of gwtc_analysis, quote the median with a 90% credible interval instead: the 5th to 95th percentiles of the samples, like ratio_q in the area-law mode or viewing_angle_low_90/high_90 in the counterpart mode. These intervals use no Gaussian assumption, so they stay correct for skewed posteriors such as distances and spins. For a Gaussian, the 90% interval is ±1.645σ.

9.2 "4.4σ" as a significance: how far a result is from a hypothesis

S = (⟨A_f⟩ − ⟨A_i⟩) / √(σ_f² + σ_i²)

The difference A_f − A_i has its own width √(σ_f² + σ_i²), because the two uncertainties are independent and add in quadrature. S counts how many of these widths separate the measured difference from 0, the boundary of a violation of the area law.

The value is turned into a probability using the one-sided tail of a Gaussian, P(A_f < A_i):

Significance One-sided probability of being wrong
1σ 0.16
3σ 1.3 × 10⁻³
3.4σ 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ minimum over the truncations
3.6σ 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ 220 + 221 from 6 M_f
4.4σ 5.4 × 10⁻⁶ headline result
5σ 2.9 × 10⁻⁷ the usual "discovery" threshold

So "4.4σ" means that, if the distributions were Gaussian, the probability that A_f < A_i would be about 5 in a million. It is one-sided because only one direction violates the law.

9.3 Caveats

  • The conversion assumes Gaussian distributions. Real posteriors have non-Gaussian tails, which is why the code also gives the direct P(A_f < A_i) from random pairs of samples.
  • A Bayesian σ is not the same as the detection significance of a GW search, which is a false-alarm rate measured on background noise. The 4.4σ here is about a physical law, not about whether the event is real.

Inspiral truncation: t< from −250 M to 0 M

  • Upper end, 0 M (the peak): the latest truncation the test allows. After the peak, the data would include the ringdown, used to measure A_f, and the two measurements would no longer be independent.
  • Lower end, −250 M (≈ 88 ms before the peak): truncating earlier throws away the loud last cycles. Most of the signal-to-noise ratio is in those final cycles, since the amplitude grows toward the merger. With less data, A_i gets wide and the test loses power: only ≈ 3.4σ at −250 M.
  • Spacing: coarse at the far end (every 50 M), dense near the peak (every 5 M from −50 M). The question is how close to the merger the inspiral-only description can be pushed, and the significance changes fastest there.

Ringdown start: t> from 6 M_f to 20 M_f

  • Lower end, 6 M_f: just after the peak, the signal is not yet a sum of a few quasinormal modes. The merger is strongly nonlinear, and many overtones contribute. Even 220 + 221 is only considered reliable from about 6 M_f, and 220 alone needs a later start: hence 10.5 M_f for the 220 headline.
  • Upper end, 20 M_f (≈ 7 ms): the ringdown decays exponentially, with a damping time of about 12 M_f. Between 10.5 and 20 M_f the amplitude falls by roughly e−9.5/12 ≈ 0.45, so A_f gets wide and the significance drops to about 1.5σ. Later starts would add little.
  • Spacing: steps of 1 M_f, plus 9.5 and 10.5 around the reference start.

The gap between the two windows

With the headline choice, the data between −40 M and +10.5 M_f (about 14 ms before the peak and 3.5 ms after it) are used by neither measurement. That stretch is the merger itself, where neither description (inspiral or quasinormal modes) is valid, and skipping it makes A_i and A_f truly independent.

The trade-off shows in the two panels of the scan plot: pushing either window toward the peak adds signal and raises the significance, but moves into the region where the model (inspiral-only, or a few quasinormal modes) is less trustworthy.

11.1 The question

Does the signal of GW190728_064510 look as if the two black holes merged inside a cloud of an ultralight scalar field? The paper reports tentative evidence for it. The mode refits the public strain data with two models and compares them:

11.2 The physics in four steps

1. An ultralight boson. Some extensions of the Standard Model predict very light particles (axion-like), with masses around 10⁻¹³–10⁻¹¹ eV. Such a light particle has a quantum wavelength of kilometres, comparable to the size of a stellar-mass black hole. The ratio of the two is the key parameter:

α = G M m_φ / (ħ c) (black-hole size / boson wavelength)

For M = 20 M_☉, α = 0.1 corresponds to m_φ ≈ 6.5 × 10⁻¹³ eV.

2. A cloud around the binary. The field forms a bound structure, like an atom's electron cloud around the nucleus ("gravitational atom"), of radius

r_φ = GM / (c² α²)

The paper and the code write it r_φ = M/α², in units where G = c = 1: there M stands for the length GM/c² (section 8), and α is dimensionless, so r_φ is a length. In SI the formula is the Bohr radius of a gravitational atom: replacing α by its definition,

r_φ = ħ² / (G M m_φ²) = λ̄_φ / α, with λ̄_φ = ħ/(m_φ c) the boson's reduced Compton wavelength,

the same as the hydrogen Bohr radius a₀ = ħ²/(m_e k e²) with the electric coupling k e² replaced by the gravitational one G M m_φ. For α = 0.19 and M ≈ 20 M_☉: GM/c² ≈ 29.5 km, so r_φ ≈ 29.5 km / 0.036 ≈ 28 GM/c² ≈ 820 km. The cloud is described by its mean density ρ̄_φ (g/cm³). The best fit for GW190728 has ρ̄_φ ≈ 6 × 10⁶ g/cm³: very dense, but the cloud is so small that its total mass is only about 10⁻⁴ of the binary's mass.

The notation ρ̄_φ. ρ is the density, the subscript φ says it's the density of the scalar field φ (not of ordinary matter), and the bar means "mean": ρ̄_φ is the mean density of the field inside the radius r_φ. A bound cloud's density varies with distance, like the electron density of an atom, peaking near the centre and falling off outside r_φ. The model doesn't use the profile in detail: it describes the cloud by this single number, and the torque formula is written in terms of it. The docs use the same notation (ρ̄_φ) as the paper, which defines it after its Eq. 2 as "the average mass density of scalars in the region r ≲ r_φ" (checked against the PDF). The code keeps the bar in the names: rhobar_phi in the sampling parameters, rhobar_cgs / rhobar in the functions, in g/cm³ (result files written with the earlier rho_phi are still read).

"Mean" is meant loosely. The code computes the cloud mass as ρ̄_φ r_φ³ (cloud_mass_fraction() in scalar_cloud.py), without the 4π/3 of a sphere's volume, so ρ̄_φ works as a density scale for the cloud rather than an exact volume average. That doesn't change the fit, since ρ̄_φ is just the amplitude of the torque. It matters when comparing the cloud mass, or the 0.1 M cap of the agnostic prior, with other papers. A factor 4π/3 ≈ 4.2 would also be the size of the "2 to 4 times lower density" difference with the paper (section 11.8); how the paper normalizes it hasn't been checked, so this is only a possibility.

How would such a cloud form? A spinning black hole can amplify waves of a bosonic field and transfer its spin to them (superradiance), growing a cloud spontaneously when α is around 0.1. This motivates the "superradiance" prior below.

3. The binary loses energy to the cloud. As the two black holes orbit inside the cloud, they stir the field and kick part of it out (ionization, like ionizing an atom). This removes orbital angular momentum, on top of what gravitational waves remove. The ratio of the two losses is δ:

δ = (cloud loss) / (GW loss), which grows as about v−13.5

v is the orbital velocity (v = (πGMf/c³)1/3 for GW frequency f). Since δ grows hugely when v is small, the effect is strongest at low frequency, early in the observed inspiral, when the orbit is wide and slow. Near the merger the GW loss dominates and the cloud doesn't matter.

4. The signal gets a phase shift. With the extra loss, the binary spirals in faster: it spends less time at each frequency. The waveform's phase then differs from vacuum by ΔΨ(f), the dephasing. The model fixes ΔΨ = 0 at the end of the inspiral (M f_c = 0.018), so the merger and ringdown are unchanged, and the difference grows toward low frequencies. Each mode (ℓ, m) of the waveform is multiplied by ei ΔΨℓm. With ρ̄_φ = 0, the cloud waveform is exactly the vacuum one (tested in the code).

Example: ρ̄_φ = 10⁶ g/cm³ and α = 0.1 dephase GW190728 by about 1.6 rad at 20 Hz and 0.13 rad at 30 Hz. The best fit has 38 rad at 20 Hz.

So the cloud model has two extra parameters, ρ̄_φ and α, on top of the ~15 binary parameters.

11.3 Background notions used below

  • Likelihood L: how well a waveform matches the data, given the noise. The code uses the log-likelihood ratio ln L (signal against noise only); ln L ≈ SNR²/2 for the best template. For GW190728, ln L ≈ 92, i.e. SNR ≈ 13.5.
  • PSD (power spectral density): the noise level of each detector against frequency, taken from the PE file. It weights each frequency in the likelihood.
  • Best fit (maximum likelihood): the parameters that maximize L. Found with a local optimizer, Nelder–Mead (scipy).
  • Nested sampling (bilby + dynesty): explores the whole prior to give the posterior samples and the evidence Z = ∫ L × prior. "Live points" set its resolution; more is slower and more accurate.
  • Chirp mass 𝓜: the mass combination that controls the inspiral speed; the best-measured mass.

11.4 Test 1: does the cloud fit better? (the statistic 𝒟)

partly the paper The paper reports the median likelihood gain over its posterior samples (Δln L ≈ 3.7, Supplemental Material, Fig. 8 inset) and a cloud posterior at a lower chirp mass (Fig. 8); it gives no best fit. The deviance 𝒟 = 2Δln L between best fits and the seeded best-fit procedure are gwtc_analysis's.

𝒟 = 2 (ln L_cloud − ln L_vacuum) at the two best fits

Its name: the deviance 𝒟, the likelihood-ratio test statistic (Neyman–Pearson), called TS in gamma-ray astronomy. "LR" is the likelihood ratio L̂_cloud / L̂_vacuum, and 𝒟 is twice its logarithm. Earlier versions of the mode called it Λ; the result files keep the key Lambda. In population analyses Λ means the hyperparameters, p_pop(θ | Λ), which is why the statistic was renamed. The terms to look up for what follows: nested models, Wilks' theorem (𝒟 ~ χ²_k under the null hypothesis, k the number of extra parameters), Chernoff's boundary case (a parameter at the edge of its range), Davies' problem or the look-elsewhere effect (a parameter undefined under the null), and the empirical (Monte Carlo) p-value used as the remedy (Test 2).

𝒟 is always ≥ 0: the cloud model contains the vacuum one (ρ̄_φ = 0), so it can always do at least as well. Two extra parameters will always absorb some noise. So a positive 𝒟 means nothing by itself: you need to know what 𝒟 noise alone gives (Test 2). The usual χ² rule doesn't apply either, because ρ̄_φ = 0 sits at the edge of its range and α does nothing when ρ̄_φ = 0.

The difficulty: finding the cloud best fit. The likelihood has two separate peaks:

  • the vacuum peak;
  • a cloud peak. The cloud posterior sits at a lower chirp mass: 9.69 against 10.14 M_☉ in the paper (Fig. 8), 9.89 against 10.14 M_☉ in the mode's samplings (detector frame). A lower chirp mass makes the inspiral slower, the cloud makes it faster, so the two compensate and the waveform still matches the data near the merger while changing at low frequency. The mode's cloud best fit (10.11 M_☉) is not in the bulk of that posterior: a fit finds the highest point, the posterior weighs the volume.

An optimizer started at the vacuum best fit stays on the vacuum peak. The first version of the mode did that (the profile step: a grid of α and of dephasings around the vacuum fit) and found only 𝒟 = 0.96: it missed the cloud peak. The current version takes its starting points from the nested sampling with the superradiance prior, which explores the whole space and does find the cloud peak:

  1. take the 25 highest-likelihood samples of that sampling;
  2. for each, adjust the merger time (the sampling marginalizes over it, the fits need a fixed one) with a fine scan;
  3. keep the 4 best as seeds, and run two Nelder–Mead passes from each with all the parameters free.

This is the seeded stage. It gives 𝒟 = 8.98 for GW190728:

ln L ratio
vacuum best fit 91.81 𝓜 = 10.07 M_☉ (detector frame), q = 0.55
cloud best fit 96.30 𝓜 = 10.11 M_☉, ρ̄_φ = 5.8 × 10⁶ g/cm³, α = 0.19 (m_φ ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻¹² eV)

11.5 Test 2: how often does noise give such a 𝒟? (the background)

added by gwtc_analysis Not in the paper: no published value to compare with.

  1. Take 150 stretches of real detector noise, off-source: within an hour of the event, at least 64 s away from it, with the same length.
  2. In each, inject the vacuum best fit: a pure vacuum signal, by construction without a cloud.
  3. Run exactly the same procedure (vacuum fit, seeded cloud fits) and compute 𝒟.
  4. The p-value is (k + 1)/(n + 1), with k of the n segments giving 𝒟 ≥ 8.98.

Result: 2 of 151 (the event and one segment with a loud noise transient, 𝒟 = 112), so p ≈ 0.013 (0.007 without that segment). 147 of 150 segments give 𝒟 < 4.1. So the event's 𝒟 is unusual for pure noise around a vacuum signal, at about the 1% level.

Caveat: the seeds come from the event's own posterior. The background therefore asks how often noise produces such an improvement in that region of parameter space, not anywhere. The p-value is in that sense optimistic.

11.6 Test 3: what would a real cloud give? (sensitivity)

added by gwtc_analysis Not in the paper: no published value to compare with.

The reverse check: inject the cloud best fit into the same 150 noise segments and rerun.

Result: 𝒟 ≈ 25 (5–95%: 9.9–44), with ρ̄_φ and α recovered without bias. Only 4.7% of these injections give 𝒟 ≤ 8.98.

So the event sits between two distributions: above what noise gives around vacuum (p ≈ 0.01), and below what this cloud would give (≈ 5%). A weaker cloud, or a noise fluctuation on a vacuum signal, fits both better than the best-fit cloud.

11.7 Test 4: the Bayes factor (the paper's actual claim)

the paper Its Bayes factors, priors (SM Eqs. 9–10) and sampler settings.

Nested sampling computes the evidence Z of each model, and the Bayes factor B = Z_cloud / Z_vacuum. Unlike 𝒟, the evidence penalizes extra parameters: it averages the likelihood over the prior, so a model that fits better only in a tiny corner of a wide prior is penalized (Occam's razor). That's why the Bayes factor depends on the prior on (ρ̄_φ, α). Two priors are used:

  • agnostic: α uniform, ρ̄_φ uniform up to 10⁹ g/cm³. The paper doesn't give the upper end, and the result depends on it strongly.
  • superradiance: only clouds that could have grown by superradiance before the merger (taking τ_d = 10⁶ yr between black-hole formation and merger) and not have decayed, with ρ̄_φ below the saturation density. This physical prior is much narrower, so it penalizes the cloud less.
here paper
ln B, superradiance 1.34 ± 0.30 3.5
ln B, agnostic (ρ̄_φ ≤ 10⁹) −0.82 ± 0.31 0.4

ln B > 0 favours the cloud; ln B ≈ 1 is weak, ln B ≈ 3 moderate. The agnostic value rises as the upper end of the prior is lowered: −0.82 at 10⁹, 0.34 at 3 × 10⁸, 1.90 at 5 × 10⁷ g/cm³. The paper's 0.4 matches an upper end near 3 × 10⁸ g/cm³.

11.12 Superradiance

Superradiance is not a counterpart. A counterpart (counterpart and h0_bright modes) is an electromagnetic signal from the same event, light seen by telescopes, as for GW170817 (kilonova, gamma-ray burst, afterglow), which identified the host galaxy and gave the redshift. Superradiance is a physical process around a spinning black hole. A scalar cloud is made of a field that doesn't interact with light, so it is dark, and GW190728 is a binary black hole merger, not expected to emit light anyway. The only other signal a cloud gives is gravitational: the continuous wave at 2 m_φ c²/h (section 11.12.3).

The process. A wave of a bosonic field passing near a rotating black hole can come out amplified, taking energy and spin from it. This happens when the wave rotates more slowly than the horizon, ω < m Ω_H (Ω_H the horizon's angular velocity, m the wave's azimuthal number). For an ultralight boson with α ≈ 0.1 the waves stay bound to the black hole, so the amplification repeats and a cloud grows spontaneously, like a laser with the black hole's rotation as the energy source.

It slows the black hole's spin. The cloud grows by taking the black hole's rotational energy and angular momentum. As the black hole slows, Ω_H falls, and the growth stops when Ω_H ≈ ω/m: the black hole keeps a reduced critical spin, set by α, and the cloud reaches its saturation mass, up to about 10% of the black hole's mass.

Phase Time scale What happens
growth τ_SR, short (from minutes to ~Myr depending on α and the mass) the cloud grows exponentially and the black hole spins down
saturation at τ_SR the spin reaches its critical value, the cloud its maximum mass
decay τ_ann, long the bosons annihilate into gravitons (the continuous wave), and the cloud slowly disappears

In scalar_cloud, superradiance enters only through the superradiance prior (SM Eqs. 9–10 of the paper). It keeps only clouds with τ_SR ≤ τ_d ≤ τ_ann, τ_d being the delay between the black hole's formation and the merger: the cloud had time to grow, and not yet to decay. That gives the window on α, 0.02 x1/9 ≤ α/(1+q) ≤ 0.11 x1/15; the saturation mass gives the upper bound on the density.

11.12.1 Constraints on the boson outside scalar_cloud

scalar_cloud looks for the effect of a cloud on one binary's inspiral. Other analyses look for the consequences superradiance would have wherever black holes exist. The references and numbers below are from memory: check them before quoting them.

1. Black hole spins. If a boson of mass m_φ exists, every black hole with α around 0.1 spins down quickly. So a black hole measured to spin fast, in the right mass range and old enough for the cloud to have grown, excludes that boson mass.

  • X-ray binaries: stellar black holes accreting from a companion; their spins come from X-ray spectra (inner edge of the disk, shape of the iron line). Cygnus X-1, for example, spins above 0.95. Arvanitaki, Baryakhtar & Huang 2015 excluded scalars of about 6 × 10⁻¹³ to 2 × 10⁻¹¹ eV.
  • GW events: the spins measured in binary black hole mergers. Ng et al. 2021 used the GWTC-2 spins to exclude about 1.3–2.7 × 10⁻¹³ eV from memory. The paper points to GW190517, whose spin measurement disfavours boson masses below about 1.1 × 10⁻¹² eV (see 11.12.2).
  • Supermassive black holes (e.g. M87*, imaged by the EHT) constrain much lighter bosons, around 10⁻²¹–10⁻²⁰ eV.

2. Continuous gravitational waves from a single cloud. A saturated cloud emits a nearly monochromatic wave at f = 2 m_φ c²/h for thousands to millions of years. All-sky searches look for a nearby unknown black hole with a cloud (the LVK O3 boson-cloud search, 20–610 Hz, shown by the continuous_waves mode); directed searches look toward known black holes such as Cygnus X-1. Nothing has been detected: the upper limits become excluded boson masses only for an assumed black hole (distance, mass, initial spin, age).

3. Stochastic background from all the clouds. The clouds around all the black holes of the Universe would add up to a background of gravitational waves. LVK upper limits on the isotropic background exclude ranges around 10⁻¹³ eV (Tsukada et al. 2019 for scalars, 2021 for vectors), again under assumptions about the black-hole population. That is the topic of the stochastic mode, whose docs don't treat bosons.

The X-ray exclusions it considers weaker, because the spins are model-dependent. Other ways out, not argued by the paper: the black hole must be old enough for the cloud to have grown, and a boson with self-interactions (as an axion with a small decay constant) can stop the growth early, leaving the spin high.

11.12.3 The continuous-wave link

The posterior boson mass, m_φ ≈ 0.89 (0.70–1.17) × 10⁻¹² eV with the superradiance prior, would make an isolated spinning black hole's cloud emit at 2 m_φ c²/h ≈ 340–570 Hz, inside the band of the LVK all-sky search for boson clouds. The continuous_waves mode draws its exclusions with this band.

12H0, redshift and distance

12.1 Two different quantities

Redshift z is how much the light (or the GW signal) was stretched on its way:

1 + z = λ_observed / λ_emitted = a(today) / a(at emission)

where a is the scale factor, the "size" of the Universe. z says how much the Universe has expanded since emission. It doesn't depend on H0 and is measured directly, from a spectrum.

H0 is how fast the Universe expands today, in km/s per Mpc. At other times the expansion rate is H(z) = H0 · E(z), with E(z) = √(Ω_m(1+z)³ + 1 − Ω_m) in flat ΛCDM.

The link is the distance. H0 sets the scale of distances for a given redshift:

D_L(z) = (1+z) (c/H0) ∫0z dz′/E(z′) ≈ c z / H0 for z ≪ 1 (Hubble's law, v = cz = H0 d)

With z alone the distance is known only in units of c/H0; with z and D_L measured independently, H0 follows. Examples, flat ΛCDM with Ω_m = 0.3065 (computed with astropy):

D_L z for H0 = 67.9 z for H0 = 73.0
40 Mpc (GW170817) 0.0090 0.0097
1000 Mpc 0.198 0.212
5000 Mpc 0.780 0.828

At a fixed distance, a larger H0 means a larger redshift: the Universe expands faster, so the light has been stretched more by the time it covers the same distance. At high redshift Ω_m (through E(z)) matters too, which is why the spectral siren has to fix or fit it.

12.2 We need z from outside the GW signal

The GW signal gives D_L (with no distance ladder) but not z. The expansion stretches every time scale by (1+z): a binary of masses m at redshift z gives exactly the signal of a binary of masses (1+z)m at rest. The detector measures m_det = (1+z) m_src and cannot separate the true mass from the redshift. So H0 needs a redshift from somewhere else:

Siren Where z comes from Mode Strength and weakness
Bright an electromagnetic counterpart identifies the host galaxy, whose spectrum gives z (corrected for its peculiar velocity, section 2) hubble_constant --method bright (GW170817) one event gives a direct H0, but counterparts are rare: only binaries with a neutron star, and close enough
Dark every galaxy of a catalog inside the GW sky area and distance range is a possible host; their redshifts are averaged over statistically hubble_constant --method dark works for any event, but each constrains H0 weakly, and the catalogs are incomplete far away
Spectral the population's mass distribution has features at known source-frame masses (the peak near 35 M☉, the cutoffs); comparing them with the measured m_det = (1+z) m_src gives z statistically hubble_constant --method spectral uses all binary black holes, but relies on the assumed shape of the mass distribution

A fourth route, not in the package: for binaries with neutron stars, the tidal deformation depends on the true mass through the equation of state, which breaks the mass–redshift degeneracy, so a single event could give z on its own (Messenger & Read 2012). It needs much more sensitive detectors (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer).

12.3 H0 once z is known

Invert the distance–redshift relation:

  • nearby source (z ≪ 1, Hubble's law): H0 ≈ c z / D_L;
  • any redshift (flat ΛCDM, Ω_m fixed): H0 = (1 + z) · c / D_L · ∫0z dz′ / √(Ω_m(1+z′)³ + 1 − Ω_m).

D_L is exactly proportional to 1/H0 at fixed z and Ω_m, so the inversion is a division (counterpart.py, implied_h0()):

def implied_h0(distances, z_obs):
    """H0 that puts each distance sample at the host redshift: d_L(z_obs; H0) = d."""
    return float(luminosity_distance(z_obs, 1.0)) / np.asarray(distances, float)

luminosity_distance(z, 1.0) is D_L for H0 = 1 km/s/Mpc; divided by the measured distance, it gives H0.

GW170817, with z_H = 0.01006 and D_L ≈ 40 Mpc:

H0 ≈ 299,792 × 0.01006 / 40 ≈ 3017 / 40 ≈ 75 km/s/Mpc

The published value is 70 (+12/−8): a single D_L is not enough, because D_L is uncertain, mainly through its degeneracy with the inclination (section 1). In practice each posterior sample of D_L gives its own H0 through the formula above (implied_h0 is applied per sample); that distribution is reweighted to remove the PE distance prior and multiplied by the redshift uncertainty (h0_bright.py), which gives a posterior on H0, not a single number.

For dark and spectral sirens z is not known per event but as a distribution (over the catalog's galaxies, or set by the mass population): H0 then comes from a joint fit rather than from this formula, but the relation is still what links D_L and z.
